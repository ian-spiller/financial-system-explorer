# Educational notes

Base teaching scenario: natural real rate 1%, expected inflation 2% and a neutral nominal ECB setting of 3%. These are hypothetical inputs, not current market observations or an estimate of the euro-area natural rate.

The map is designed around a few important distinctions. These boundaries apply to the teaching examples, rather than making claims about current prices.

## Policy rates and market rates

The ECB deposit facility rate anchors short-term market conditions. It does not directly set every deposit rate, lending rate or bond yield. Expectations, maturity, credit risk, liquidity, funding and competition also matter. The overnight 3.1%, deposit 3% and other bank quotes are illustrative, not an observed market curve. Required-reserve remuneration and the required-reserve ratio are different concepts.

## Banks and money creation

Banks transform maturities and manage liquidity and repricing mismatches. A new loan normally creates a matching deposit; banks are not limited to lending prior household savings. The loan-pricing branches are alternative representations of the same debt funding basis, never additive charges. In the base case, customer deposits pay 3%, the overnight interbank reference is 3.1%, and five-year bank bonds cost the shared 3.5% reference plus a 0.5 percentage-point bank issuer spread, or 4%. With 80% deposits and 20% bank bonds, current debt funding costs 3.2%; the reconciliation to the overnight alternative is 3.2% − 3.1% = 0.1 percentage points. Part of the funding/liquidity premium is already in these source rates. Credit risk 1, the incremental liquidity buffer 0.3, operations 0.5 and capital/profit 1 add another 2.8 percentage points, giving a variable loan quote of 6%. All figures are assumptions.

## QE and reserves

In the fund-seller example, the Eurosystem creates reserve liabilities, the commercial bank receives reserves and credits the fund's deposit, and the fund exchanges a bond for a deposit. When the bank itself sells the bond, settlement does not mechanically create a customer deposit. Required reserves are only one part of bank reserve holdings; reserves are not bank equity or an automatic loan multiplier.

## Government financing

Interest is budget spending. Repaying principal is a financing transaction. Refinancing replaces maturing debt; the deficit increases the debt stock in the simplified example. The annual calculation assumes year-end issuance and refinancing, issuance at par, unchanged cash and no other debt adjustments. The feedback loop holds revenue, non-interest spending and the assumed yield constant. Greater borrowing pressure can raise yields, but the model does not impose an automatic debt-to-yield formula. The new sovereign yield uses the same five-year reference plus the selected sovereign spread: 3.5% + 0.4 percentage points = 3.9% by default. Changing its yield preset changes the same spread used in the government-bond valuation; old debt retains its existing fixed coupon.

## NPV, firm value and financing

The main company lesson follows one initial investment of 100. It produces expected after-tax operating cash of 15 each year for five years, before financing payments, and recovers the full invested capital of 100 at the end of Year 5. Capital stays invested at 100 throughout the five-year period.

The default company uses five-year corporate-bond funding: 40% target debt weight × 5% debt rate × (1 − 25% tax rate) + 60% target equity weight × 8.5% equity return = WACC 6.6%. The target debt/equity ratio is constant in market-value terms and business risk is unchanged. The historic initial outlay is still 40 debt + 60 contributed equity; project NPV accrues to equity. Alternative bank-loan funding uses the actual selected quote from Commercial banks, and all downstream calculations update.

The annual capital charge is 100 × 6.6% = 6.6. Expected operating cash of 15 less this economic opportunity-cost charge leaves annual surplus of 8.4. This is an investment hurdle, not a contractual cash payment to equity holders. The assumed interest tax shield is usable and is applied to debt once.

The present value of five annual surpluses of 8.4 at 6.6% is 34.813699. This equals standard project NPV: the present value of five operating cash flows of 15 plus Year 5 capital recovery of 100, less the initial 100. Firm value is 100 + NPV = 134.813699. Calculations use full precision and displayed figures are rounded.

There are no year-selection buttons or terminal-wealth comparison in the main company lesson. Actual reinvestment at WACC is not required to calculate DCF NPV. Figures are rounded for display; all calculations use unrounded values. Forecast NPV is not a guaranteed realised gain.

## Bonds and equities

For unchanged fixed bond payments, a higher required yield reduces their present value. Both corporate and sovereign bonds use the shared five-year nominal reference, with one issuer spread added. The corporate spread is decomposed into credit and liquidity contributions in the fixed illustrative 2:1 proportion; it is not stacked on the sovereign spread. Equity valuation and the company cost of equity use the same three-factor expected-return example: shared reference + market beta × market premium + value beta × value premium + size beta × size premium. The dividend model retains constant 2% growth and applies the shared reference as a flat long-horizon valuation proxy; its intrinsic value need not equal a traded price.

## Funds, benchmarks and alpha

Goals, constraints and desired exposures come before implementation. An index fund can passively track a value index while retaining value exposure. Active allocation can use passive funds. The allocation weights are hypothetical examples, not an optimised personal recommendation.

Benchmark outperformance and model alpha answer different questions. The return example evaluates only the equity sleeve. Its market, value and size contributions are synthetic, not estimates from a regression. FF3 adds size and value to the market; an optional FF5 explanation assumes zero additional loadings here. One period's residual is not an estimated alpha. Matching an intended factor exposure is consistent with a plan, not proof of repeatable skill; an unintended exposure does not by itself prove pure luck. The visible default assumes zero management fees, so its gross and net gaps coincide. If a nonzero fee is supplied, the factor ladder explains the gross gap and the selected-model equation deducts the fee to reconcile to the net gap. Benchmark-relative factor contributions use portfolio minus benchmark loadings. The model rows are attributions of the benchmark gap, not standalone regression alphas for the fund. Fees and benchmark specification matter.

## Households and consumption through time

The household example uses real income of €10,000 today and €10,000 next year. The willingness-to-wait comparison remains quoted per additional €1. Increasing the budget from €100 to €10,000 changes the monetary scale, not preferences, annual returns or the consumption shares. All market rates remain linked to Interest rates.

The personal starting required real return is 1%, matching the neutral market real return of 1% for the default teaching baseline. Optimal consumption is then €10,000 in each period, with no transfer. This personal preference is an independent input, not a rate set by the ECB or an identity equating every household’s preference with the natural rate. Personal presets remain 0%, 1% and 2%. Market controls use the shared Neutral, Cooling and Supporting scenarios, with annual real returns of 1%, 2% and 0%.

The starting threshold equals MRS minus one at the income point (10,000, 10,000). With log utility log(C_today) + beta × log(C_later), beta = 1 / starting MRS. Saving s changes consumption to 10,000 − s today and 10,000 + (1 + r) × s next year. At the optimum the constant-utility curve is tangent to the budget line: MRS = 1 + r. CFA’s discounted future/current marginal-utility convention is the reciprocal under certainty.

The consumption-shift graphic compares each current optimum with the Neutral 1% market rate at the same personal preference. It shows the reference and current amounts, their exact difference, and a visibly enlarged bar scale labelled ±€100 for the three policy scenarios. This is a scale for the change, not a claim that €50 is a large share of the entire budget. The reference stays fixed when switching directly between Cooling and Supporting. With the default 1% personal threshold, Cooling gives current consumption €9,951.22 and next-year consumption €10,049.75; Supporting gives €10,049.75 and €9,950.25. These amounts use full-precision calculations before rounding.

The classic MRS graph retains the full budget, with both axes fixed at 0–25,000. Its dashed curves and hollow point represent the same Neutral comparison, rather than the last button clicked. Borrowing is repaid from next year’s income, and maximum borrowing with zero future consumption is 10,000 / (1 + r). That endpoint is not optimal under log utility. At a 0% market rate and 2% starting threshold, optimal borrowing is about €99.01, current consumption €10,099.01 and future consumption €9,900.99; initial MRS 1.02 falls to 1 at the optimum.

Income and payoffs are certain and real. Saving and borrowing use the same rate, with no fees or separate credit constraints. This is a consumption-choice illustration, not an actual commercial-bank loan quote or a personal investment recommendation. Risky investment payoffs also involve uncertainty, risk and liquidity.

## Shared Interest rates overview

The expandable panel is a separate stylised macro example at potential output. It keeps six participant nodes; the overview is a mechanism, not a seventh institution. Its conceptual arrows are not cash payments. It links the household's individual saving/borrowing choice to an aggregate private-saving schedule, and company investment and public deficits to financing demand. Private saving also includes retained business saving. A single household's MRS does not mechanically determine aggregate supply.

In illustrative units, S_private(r) = 50 + 25 × r + saving_shift, I(r) = 90 − 25 × r + investment_shift, and the public deficit is 10 in the base case. Solving S_private = I + deficit gives r* = 1% and financing of 75 (company investment 65 plus deficit 10). The visible Base scenario holds the public deficit at 10. The internal model also supports isolated saving and investment shifts of 40 for consistency checks. They are isolated comparisons with fixed potential income, fixed expectations and no foreign flows. Refinancing maturing debt is not added as new net demand here. Government borrowing does not impose a universal automatic debt-to-yield rule.

“Real” describes inflation adjustment, rather than a separate policy instrument: the natural rate is also a real rate. In New Keynesian theory, the natural real rate is the rate in flexible-price equilibrium and can vary with real shocks. Natural and neutral terminology can differ across models and horizons; this illustration uses one real reference r*. The comparison is real policy > r*: restrictive; equal: neutral; below: accommodative. Changing r* can change the stance even at an unchanged nominal policy setting.

The dashed policy line is the approximate real ECB setting: nominal policy minus expected inflation. The base case is neutral: 3% nominal − 2% expected inflation = 1% real, matching r*. The named controls select a demand-shock example and the ECB response together. The comparison is educational, not a complete policy rule: actual r* is unobserved and uncertain. The household lesson uses a one-year real consumption-choice benchmark. Its default market return follows the shared short real base. The household market controls now select the shared Neutral, Cooling and Supporting scenarios; its personal required real return remains a separate choice. The frictionless certain-rate budget uses one saving and borrowing rate, rather than treating bank borrowing or risky equity returns as certain household income.

Interest rates and the ECB card share three illustrative scenarios. In Interest rates, a single control above both rate cards selects the demand shock and policy response together; Neutral also restores the base case. The natural-rate card shows the current equilibrium rate, with its pre-shock change on a separate line. Neutral: natural real 1%, real policy 1%, nominal ECB 3%. Cooling: firms increase planned investment; the financing-demand curve shifts right and the assumed natural rate rises from 1% to 1.1%. Before the ECB responds, unchanged real policy of 1% is below this new natural rate. With a pre-existing positive output gap and inflation pressure, the example ECB response takes real policy to 2% and nominal policy to 4%. Supporting reverses the assumed shock: natural falls to 0.9%, initial real policy of 1% is restrictive, and the response lowers real policy to 0% and nominal policy to 2%. Expected inflation stays at 2% in every case. The ±0.1 percentage-point natural-rate changes and the policy responses are teaching assumptions, not magnitudes inferred from the output gap. A positive or negative output gap does not mechanically imply a particular change in r*.

The ECB card follows time order: investment demand → natural-rate shift and the initial real-policy comparison → output gap and inflation pressure → the ECB response. One calculation then links real policy + expected inflation ≈ nominal ECB → overnight → variable bank loan. Neutral is 1% + 2% ≈ 3% → 3.1% → 6%; Cooling is 2% + 2% ≈ 4% → 4.1% → 7%; Supporting is 0% + 2% ≈ 2% → 2.1% → 5%. The loan comes from the actual shared bank funding and charge calculation. The initial output gaps of +5%, −5% or 0% are assumed starting conditions, not outcomes computed from the 0.1 percentage-point natural-rate changes. Demand-driven inflation pressure is shown while expected inflation is held anchored at 2%. Transmission takes time and depends on expectations, persistence and other factors.

The final ECB graphic is a schematic adjustment toward a zero output gap. During this adjustment, the post-shock natural rate is assumed to remain at its new level: 1.1% in Cooling, 0.9% in Supporting or 1% in Neutral. Real policy returns toward that current natural rate as the gap closes: 2% → 1.1%, 0% → 0.9% or 1% → 1%. If the underlying shock later fades, the natural rate may move again. The graphic provides no intermediate forecasts, time units or calibrated speed, and does not solve a dynamic IS/Phillips-curve model. Output and inflation persistence motivate a temporary policy response beyond merely matching the new natural rate. This is an internally consistent illustrative sequence, not a claim that every demand shock calls for the same response.

The nominal pricing inputs are linked across Interest rates, Banks, Companies, government financing, bond valuation and equity valuation. The base is built once: short real rate + expected inflation + term premium. The simple ECB control changes the flat short nominal assumption alongside current policy, holding inflation and spreads constant; this makes the pricing response visible without forecasting a multi-year path. Actual long rates need not move one-for-one with current policy. The example also assumes full immediate deposit pass-through and a 0.1 percentage-point interbank gap. Funds attribution concerns synthetic realised performance rather than these forward-looking required returns.

The natural real rate r* plus expected inflation gives an approximate neutral nominal reference. Current policy can be above or below neutral; the starting point is set equal to it for clarity. In the flat short-rate example, the nominal base equals the ECB setting, without the separate 0.1 percentage-point interbank quote spread. The reference is a stylised default-free rate; bank-specific funding spreads are added separately. Inflation and the term premium enter once. When an already observed maturity-matched yield is used, its embedded components must not be added again.

The Interest rates overview and company/equity-valuation models use identical market, value and size exposures and expected premia. Funds uses the same expected premia with the equity sleeve's chosen exposures: active value-plus-size starts at 8.5%; passive broad-market implementation has no value/size tilts and requires 7.5%. Its forward-looking pricing examples are separate from the synthetic realized-return attribution below. Changing today's rate scenario does not rewrite that historical example. The corporate bond branch and detailed corporate valuation use the same aggregate issuer spread. These expected factor contributions are model assumptions, not guarantees or realised alpha; the separate Funds attribution example evaluates ex post performance.

### Required-return building blocks

The numerical inputs occupy the first row; the shared five-year base branches down to separate bond and equity boxes on the next row, without horizontal scrolling: 1% short real base + 2% expected inflation + 0.5 percentage-point term premium = 3.5% five-year nominal reference. The 3% ECB setting matches the neutral nominal reference. Bond credit 1 and liquidity 0.5 percentage points give a 5% corporate yield; this credit contribution includes expected-default-loss compensation as well as risk compensation. Equity market beta 1 × premium 4%, value beta 0.5 × premium 1% and size beta 0.5 × premium 1% contribute 5 percentage points, giving 8.5% cost of equity. The equity factor rows are exposure-weighted contributions and do not stack the corporate-bond credit spread onto equity. There are no annual expectation bars, recovery/cooling paths or averaging controls.

The horizontal example explicitly fixes the market-reference horizon at five years. Its term premium compensates for duration/interest-rate risk relative to rolling short instruments across that horizon; it is distinct from pure compensation for waiting and from issuer credit or liquidity components. Term premia are estimated, can be negative, and need not rise mechanically with maturity. The example adds its positive 0.5 percentage-point term premium once in the five-year reference. The separate risk-premia box now shows credit +1 pp, liquidity +0.5 pp, and expected equity factor contributions: market +4 pp, value +0.5 pp, size +0.5 pp. The equity rows are exposure-weighted contributions, not raw factor premia: market 1×4%, value 0.5×1%, size 0.5×1%. Group totals and final required returns remain unchanged.

## Coverage

The six-participant map is intentionally selective. Insurance, derivatives, payment infrastructure and cross-border/FX channels are not separate main nodes. Connections are schematic relationships, not measured flow volumes. Primary issuance funds issuers; secondary trading pays an existing holder.

### Visible company financing build-up

The company's default two branches both begin at the common 3.5% five-year reference. Corporate bonds add credit 1 and liquidity 0.5 percentage points to reach 5%; equity adds the same three-factor contributions as Interest rates to reach 8.5%. The 40/60 tax-adjusted financing weights produce 1.5 + 5.1 = WACC 6.6%. Selecting Bank loan instead uses the actual bank quote and its source-specific costs. A variable loan uses the current overnight reference; a five-year fixed loan uses the same five-year reference as bonds and equity.

For the bank's fixed quote, only its 80% floating-funded slice is hedged; its 20% five-year bond funding is already fixed. The shared fixed benchmark 3.5% minus the short floating reference 3.1% is 0.4 percentage points. The weighted hedge cost is 80% × 0.4 = 0.32 percentage points. Current funding 3.2% + hedge 0.32 = fixed funding 3.52%; plus unchanged loan charges 2.8 gives a 6.32% fixed loan. Its fixed funding basis relative to the shared 3.5% reference is 0.02 percentage points, which is displayed once. The swap is assumed to use the same floating reference, the deposit/reference basis is constant, the floating slice is equally sized and fully hedgeable, the bank-bond term matches the five-year lock, and all credit, liquidity and other costs are held unchanged. This is a simplified matched-funding illustration, not a twenty-year rate lock or a risk-free funding claim.


### Interpretation of the simple building blocks

The default uses a neutral, flat short-rate scenario, rather than a forecast. Away from neutrality, the market short real base can differ from the estimated natural rate. Under the illustrative ECB control the short real base is the chosen flat nominal level less expected inflation. In the detailed bond view a separate short-nominal preset is an alternative market assumption; it does not change the current ECB setting.

Term premium compensates for bearing longer-horizon interest-rate/inflation uncertainty rather than merely waiting longer. The assumed 0.5 percentage-point premium is not estimated and need not always be positive. Expected inflation compensation is distinct from uncertainty about inflation. The simplified term-risk allowance does not separately estimate an inflation-risk premium. The additive decomposition approximates exact compounding. The common five-year reference used for equity valuation is a teaching proxy, not an assertion that equities mature in five years.

### Origin and estimation of the term premium

The card treats 0.5 percentage points as a teaching assumption, not an estimate. The long reference is constructed as 3% plus this assumed premium. Subtracting 3% from that constructed 3.5% would only recover the assumption, so the interface does not present that subtraction as a calculation of the premium.

Economically, the premium is market compensation for bearing longer-horizon interest-rate risk. Its level depends on uncertainty, the amount of duration risk the private sector must absorb, investors’ risk-bearing capacity and demand for long bonds. Central-bank bond purchases can reduce the duration risk held by investors and compress the premium. The premium need not be positive.

Empirically, observed long yields can be decomposed using models and/or surveys to estimate the expected short-rate component over the relevant horizon. This component and the term premium are not directly observable. The yield-minus-expected-short-rate relation defines the decomposition; it does not independently explain an assumed numerical premium. Today’s ECB setting alone is insufficient to identify it.

## Numerical display and navigation consistency

The equity-valuation detail preserves its origin: a Funds link uses the sleeve's current factor loadings; a Companies or Interest rates link uses the company example. Both use the same expected premia and market reference. Thus passive broad-market pricing remains 7.5% in both the allocation card and its valuation detail, while the default value-and-size company example remains 8.5%. The central-bank variable-loan link selects that same variable contract at the bank. Funds shows current required returns separately from an illustrative past period: a market return of 8%, value-factor return of 4% and size-factor return of 2% are realized teaching inputs, rather than today's expected premia. Each corporate issuer spread is split consistently into two-thirds credit and one-third liquidity compensation; this split is an assumption, not an empirical estimate.

Company WACC and annual capital-charge displays retain up to three decimal places when needed, so the fixed-bank-loan contribution 40% × 6.32% × (1 − 25%) = 1.896 percentage points combines with 5.1 to give WACC 6.996%. Present values are computed at full precision. Rounded individual annual present values and the rounded NPV carry an approximation sign; adding displayed rounded values may differ by a cent. The graph's financing amounts are aggregate illustrative index units, separate from an individual company's investment of 100 or one household's income budget.
